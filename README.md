# Why Are We Here? To Learn to Judge, Not to Write
In my final-year project, my team and I are producing an AI short film. Early on, I asked an AI model to turn our script into a nine-panel storyboard. The result looked exactly right: nine frames in order, each captioned with a shot description. For a moment I thought, this is it — this is what we needed. Then we actually tried to build from it, and the trouble began. The panels did not connect. A beat that should have carried from one shot to the next simply vanished. The visual tension we wanted was missing, and I could not put my finger on why.
I am not here to write every line of code by hand. I am here to learn judgment. An AI can produce; someone still has to know whether the production is any good. That someone needs two things: a clear sense of what the work should be, and the language to say exactly where the output falls short. Neither of those comes from being able to describe what you want. Both of them come from understanding the medium.
The first thing I am learning is the gap between describing and specifying. "Give me a storyboard with more visual tension" is a description. Anybody can say it. But "visual tension" is not a parameter — it is a feeling that has to be translated into decisions about shot size, camera movement, pacing, and how one frame hands off to the next. The AI did not fail because it was careless. It failed because my request was not a specification. It could not know what tension meant for our film, because I had not turned my instinct into concrete rules. Programming, I am beginning to see, is exactly this discipline: taking a vague wish and making it precise enough for a machine to execute. Designers do this with briefs; programmers do it with code. Learning one trains the other.
