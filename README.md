# Why Are We Here? To Learn to Judge, Not to Write
In my graduation project, my team and I were creating an AI short film. At first, I had an AI model convert our script into a nine-panel storyboard script. The result looked flawless: nine frames arranged in order, each with a shot description. At that moment, I thought, "That's it—that's exactly what we need."  However, problems arose when we tried to build upon it. The storyboards lacked continuity. The rhythm that should have transitioned from one shot to the next suddenly vanished. The visual tension we wanted was missing, yet I couldn't pinpoint the reason. I believe this is precisely why I'm learning this course.
By 2026, artificial intelligence systems will be able to write executable code based on a sentence in English and generate images, storyboards, and prototypes according to requirements. They make progress every six months. So, as a designer, why should I sit in a programming classroom? I'm not here to personally write every line of code. I came here to learn judgment. Artificial intelligence can create; But ultimately, someone still needs to judge the quality of the work.
This person needs two things: a clear understanding of what the work should look like, and language that can accurately point out the shortcomings of the output results. Both of these points cannot be obtained simply by describing what you want, but stem from an understanding of the medium. The first thing I need to learn is the gap between description and specific explanation. 'Give me a more visually compelling storyboard script' is a description that anyone can say. But 'visual tension' is not a parameter - it is a feeling that must be translated into decisions about lens size, lens motion, rhythm, and how to transition the image to the next frame. Artificial intelligence failed not because it was careless, but because my request was not specific. It cannot understand what 'tension' means to our movie because I have not translated my intuition into specific rules. I am beginning to understand that programming is a discipline that transforms vague wishes into precise instructions that machines can execute. Designers achieve this through design briefs; Programmers implement it through code. Learning from one side enables better learning from the other.
The second thing is reading versus writing. If a machine writes the code, someone still has to decide whether it's any good. An AI-generated storyboard looks a lot like an AI-generated citation: confident, properly formatted, and entirely capable of being wrong. As our course glossary puts it, a bug isn't a crash; it's "code that runs, and does the wrong thing." The dangerous failures are the ones that look like successes. Catching them takes enough understanding to read the output critically — to notice that a shot doesn't follow the one before it, that a gesture is missing, that the pacing has gone flat. That's a reading skill, and it's not the same as being able to write.
And my storyboard experience points exactly here. I could feel that something was off — because I have a standard for what our film should be. But feeling that something is off isn't the same as knowing what to change. To fix the storyboard, I had to learn to say: this shot breaks the continuity, this frame carries no visual weight, this sequence needs a wider shot to breathe. Those are judgments about the medium, and they're exactly the judgments that programming trains. Understanding how a system works — whether it's a film pipeline or a piece of software — is what turns "something feels off" into "here's what to change."
So why am I here? Not because code will always be written by hand — it won't. I'm here because machines keep getting better at producing, and people who understand the medium keep getting more valuable. In an era when anyone can generate, judgment is what separates the person who actually made the work from the person who just pressed the button. My experience taught me that description is cheap and judgment isn't. If I can leave this course able to read a system, know what I want from it, and tell when it's lying to me — that's why I'm here, and that's what this course means to me.
